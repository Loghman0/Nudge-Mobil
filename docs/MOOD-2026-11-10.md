# Mood-aware questions and reviewed priorities — 10 November 2026

Mood is an optional description of **how the person feels now**. It can guide question comfort, effort and variety. It does not make an answer correct, diagnose the person, establish a stable trait or justify choosing an option for them. This feature uses deterministic shared rules, with no AI call or new mood-history database.

## Current source behavior

Eight choices are available: **Happy, Excited, Calm, Sad, Angry, Anxious, Tired and Mixed**, plus **No preference**. The native Today screen offers a brief optional prompt after authentication and any pending preview review/save. Skip, Close and Android Back proceed without a mood. Mood, pace and topic remain manually available through the settings sheet.

The automatic prompt is tracked once per device-local day and account using an encrypted local day marker. The marker stores `{version, day}`, not the emotion or an answer. The emotion remains in session memory; reopening the app does not restore it as a recorded preference. When the app returns to the foreground on a different local day, the prior mood is cleared. Unsaved/revisited answers, pending retries and preview save work defer the popup rather than interrupt the draft. If device storage cannot persist the marker, the once-per-day convenience is not guaranteed.

The selected emotion is sent in the next-question API request so the server can apply the shared selector. It is not written to the user's profile as mood history or converted into answer evidence. Ordinary operational processing still occurs; this is not a claim that no server ever receives the selection. Changing the settings requests a different question without submitting the current answer. Closing an unapplied settings draft preserves its previous values.

| Selected mood | Selection preference, after normal eligibility checks |
|---|---|
| Happy | More play and variety, with short questions |
| Excited | Playful/creative variety and simple formats |
| Calm | Some reflective formats within the already chosen pace and permissions |
| Sad | Short, concrete everyday questions with less work |
| Angry | Simple, concrete questions with less digging |
| Anxious | Short, straightforward questions |
| Tired | Less reading, writing and sorting |
| Mixed / No preference | Usual varied selection; no mood-fit bonus |

These are **product heuristics**, not prescribed psychological treatments. Mood adds a bounded ranking preference; it does not bypass topic choice, muted/optional topics, writing permission, depth limits, already answered questions, exposure novelty, forgotten evidence or pause rules. Exploration still covers under-represented domains. Sadness is not a request for sad content, anger is not evidence of aggression, and calmness is not consent to sensitive questions.

## How mood can help a decision

The shared helper offers up to three possible priorities. For example, Angry offers **Flexibility to change plans**, **Commitments required** and **Effort needed**. Happy/Excited offer enjoyment, novelty and flexibility. The person explicitly chooses any priority that matters; none is added automatically.

An accepted native suggestion becomes an ordinary editable criterion with default importance **3/5**, a **0–10 points** range and unknown option values. The helper supplies the label/direction, not option ratings, factual evidence or a winner. The person can change the scale/importance, rate the options, remove the criterion and review the final comparison. There is no rule such as “angry means choose B.”

The native popup's mood itself is not saved with a decision. A criterion deliberately accepted by the user can be saved through the normal decision draft/confirmation flow. The web app's existing explicitly selected mood field is different: it may remain a journal note saved with that decision, and the UI says the note alone does not change scores. Reviewed criteria, rather than an emotion label, provide any comparison effect.

## Research basis and limits

- An experimental experience-sampling study found longer questionnaires increased burden and compromised response quantity/quality. This supports keeping effort low, not a validated emotion-to-question formula. [Eisele et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32909448/)
- Two momentary/daily-diary studies found overlapping but nonexchangeable reports of affect, including some retrospective negative-affect bias. Current emotion should not be presented as a timeless description, and momentary ratings are not universally superior. [Neubauer et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6842033/)
- Three experiments found emotional engagement affected retrieval of opposite-mood autobiographical memories. Readily recalled material need not be balanced or more accurate just because its mood matches the person. [Greenberg and Meiran](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3916766/)

None of these studies clinically validates Nudge's eight choices, mappings or reflections. Validate whether people find the prompt optional, understandable and useful; whether skips/drop-off improve; and whether the question mix remains varied. Do not claim clinical validity, mood improvement or retention gains without the corresponding evidence.
